W Waiting: 3–7-day intake queue and three approval gates.

TIME: 1:30. MAIN MESSAGE: Two different problems hide in the same map. Long lead time comes from waiting and rework loops; limited capacity comes from manual staff hours. Fixing one does not fix the other. SPEAKING POINTS: (1) TIMWOODS: we apply only the three wastes our evidence supports. W = Waiting (intake queue 3–7 days; Clarification, Approval and Final Approval gates). O = Overprocessing (scripts drafted by hand and only then refined with AI; audio and video generated separately and synchronized manually). D = Defects/rework (each gate can send the order back; the brief loop reportedly takes at least four rounds). (2) Five Whys on the lead-time symptom ends at a specific cause: requests arrive free-form and the format is only settled after drafts, so variety is absorbed manually instead of standardized at intake. "The process does not scale" is the problem statement, not the cause. (3) Theory of Constraints: the resource that limits output is staff time; the heaviest single load on it is manual A/V sync. Approval delays sit with lawyers and extend lead time, but they do not consume HiLegal staff capacity. Faster approvals alone will not raise output; automating sync alone will barely shorten lead time. CALCULATIONS (Appendix A): the stated ceiling 35–40 equals 100% of paid hours (2 × 176 h ÷ 10 h = 35.2), so there is no slack; at a realistic 60% utilisation capacity is ~19 per month, already at today's output of 15–20 (lecture Session_1.2, slide 05: utilisation = demand ÷ capacity). Plausibility check: if each failed clarification re-enters the 3–7-day queue, three extra loops add 9–21 days, consistent with the reported 14–28 days of waiting. This is a consistency check, not a measurement. LITTLE'S LAW (plausibility only): WIP = throughput × flow time = 15–20 per month × 0.47–0.93 months ≈ 7–19 orders in progress (lecture Session_2.1, slide 3; assumes a stable system; ranges are not paired observations).
